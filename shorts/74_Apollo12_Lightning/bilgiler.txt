On November 14, 1969, Apollo 12 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center into rain and low clouds, with President Richard Nixon watching — the first U.S. president to see a crewed launch in person. About 36.5 seconds into the flight, lightning struck the Saturn V. At 52 seconds, it was hit again. There had been no natural lightning in the area; the rocket and its long, conductive exhaust plume triggered the discharges themselves.

Inside the command module nearly every warning light came on and all three fuel cells dropped offline. Commander Pete Conrad radioed that they had "everything in the world drop out." In Mission Control the telemetry turned to nonsense — until EECOM flight controller John Aaron recognised the pattern from a test about a year earlier and called "SCE to Aux," switching the Signal Conditioning Equipment to its backup supply. Almost nobody in the room knew what he meant, but Lunar Module Pilot Alan Bean found the switch, flipped it, and the data came back. The Saturn V's own guidance had kept flying a perfect course the whole time.

After checking the spacecraft in Earth orbit, the crew headed for the Moon. On November 19, Conrad and Bean landed Intrepid in the Ocean of Storms, within walking distance of the robotic Surveyor 3 probe. Controllers reportedly worried that the strikes might have damaged the parachute system, and chose not to tell the crew — but on November 24 all three parachutes opened normally.
