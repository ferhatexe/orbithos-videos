An Iron Rod Went Through His Head — and He Lived 🧠
AÇIKLAMA:
On 13 September 1848, Phineas Gage, a railroad construction foreman in Cavendish, Vermont, was tamping blasting powder into a hole when a spark set it off. The iron — about 1.1 meters long and 6 kilograms — shot upward, entered under his left cheekbone, passed behind his left eye and flew out through the top of his skull, landing many meters away.

Minutes later he was sitting up in an ox cart, talking. He survived, but his doctor, John Harlow, later wrote that his friends said he was "no longer Gage." Yet Gage went on to drive stagecoaches between Santiago and Valparaíso in Chile for years — which researchers like Malcolm Macmillan read as a sign he recovered more than the legend suggests. He died in 1860.

His skull and the tamping iron are kept together at Harvard's Warren Anatomical Museum. And in 2009, a daguerreotype surfaced showing him scarred, left eye closed, holding the iron.
