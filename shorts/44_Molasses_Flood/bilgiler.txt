AÇIKLAMA:
On January 15, 1919, a giant storage tank in Boston's North End held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, meant to be turned into industrial alcohol for munitions. The day was unusually warm for January. Shortly after noon, witnesses heard a sound like machine-gun fire as the tank's rivets shot out — and then the whole tank burst.

A brown wave, reported at up to 25 feet high, rushed through the streets at about 35 miles per hour. It crushed buildings, buckled the steel girders of the elevated railway, and swept people and horses away. 21 people died and about 150 were injured.

In 2016, researchers led by Nicole Sharp showed why it was so deadly: as the molasses spread out in the cold winter air, it became several times thicker, trapping victims and rescuers alike. The company blamed anarchists, but the court found the tank had been built too weak. For decades, locals said the harbor smelled sweet on warm days.
