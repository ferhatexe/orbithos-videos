On December 14, 1966, Jim LeBlanc, a technician and volunteer test subject at NASA's Manned Spacecraft Center in Houston, was testing a spacesuit inside a vacuum chamber that simulated an altitude of about 120,000 feet. Then the hose that kept his suit pressurized came loose, and within seconds the pressure inside his suit dropped to almost nothing.

At that pressure, water boils at body temperature. Above roughly 63,000 feet (about 19 km) — the so-called Armstrong limit — the heat of your own body is enough to boil water at the surface of the body, like the saliva on your tongue. LeBlanc later said that the last thing he remembered was feeling that saliva start to bubble. He stayed conscious for about 14 seconds, then collapsed.

His colleagues immediately began letting air back into the chamber; by most accounts it took about a minute and a half (Guinness gives 87 seconds) to bring the pressure back up. LeBlanc woke up as the pressure returned, and his only reported injury was an earache. His accident remains one of the very few real-world records of a human exposed to near vacuum.
