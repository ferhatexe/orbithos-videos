AÇIKLAMA:
On June 30, 1908, a blast over the Tunguska region of Siberia flattened an estimated 80 million trees across about 2,150 square kilometres — an area bigger than Greater London. At the Vanavara trading post, about 65 km (40 miles) away, a man named Semenov later said he felt such heat he wanted to tear off his shirt, before the blast threw him from his seat.

For several nights afterward, skies over Europe and Asia glowed so brightly that, by some reports, people could read outdoors at midnight. No scientist reached the site until Leonid Kulik's expedition in 1927. He found trees knocked down in a vast radial pattern, all pointing away from one spot — but at the center, trees were still standing, stripped of their branches like telegraph poles. There was no crater.

Most scientists think a space rock (an asteroid or possibly a comet fragment) exploded several kilometres above the ground — an airburst estimated at around 10–15 megatons, hundreds of times the Hiroshima bomb. The exact object is still debated.
