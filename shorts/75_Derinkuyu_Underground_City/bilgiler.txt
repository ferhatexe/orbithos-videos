AÇIKLAMA:
In 1963, a resident of Derinkuyu in Cappadocia, Turkey, was renovating his house when he knocked through a wall and found a hidden room. Behind it, a tunnel led down into one of the largest underground cities ever excavated, carved by hand into soft volcanic tuff.

Derinkuyu reaches roughly 85 meters below the surface, with at least eight levels open or mapped (some sources suggest more than that). Experts estimate it could shelter around 20,000 people together with their livestock and food stores. It had stables, kitchens, wine and oil presses, wells, storage rooms and chapels; ventilation shafts carried fresh air down to the deepest floors, and some wells could not be reached from the surface, so attackers could not poison the water. In times of danger, each level could be sealed with a huge rolling stone door weighing up to about half a ton, which could only be moved from the inside. A tunnel of about eight kilometers is said to connect it to the underground city of Kaymaklı, though this has not been fully confirmed.

Nobody knows exactly who started digging. The Phrygians are often credited (around the 8th–7th centuries BC); the city was greatly expanded in the Byzantine era, when Christians used it to shelter from raids, and Cappadocian Greeks were still using it until the 1923 population exchange. Then it was abandoned — and forgotten for about forty years.
