A 72-Second Signal From Space Never Came Back 📡
AÇIKLAMA:
On August 15, 1977, Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope recorded a narrowband burst from the direction of Sagittarius. It lasted about 72 seconds — exactly how long Earth's rotation took to sweep the telescope's beam across a fixed point in the sky.

Days later, astronomer Jerry Ehman read the computer printout and saw the sequence 6EQUJ5. Each character measures signal strength; the "U" means about 30 times above the background noise. He circled it in red pen and wrote "Wow!" in the margin. The signal sat near the 1420 MHz hydrogen line, a protected band where Earth transmitters are not allowed. Telescopes returned to the same spot many times. It was never detected again.

In 2024, a team led by Abel Méndez proposed a natural explanation: a cold hydrogen cloud suddenly brightened by a flare from a magnetar behind it, acting like a natural "space laser" (a maser). It is still a hypothesis. Big Ear itself was dismantled in 1998 to make room for a golf course.
