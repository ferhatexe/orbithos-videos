In July 1518, in the city of Strasbourg, a woman known as Frau Troffea began dancing in the street — and kept going for nearly a week. Others joined her: dozens, then perhaps as many as four hundred, dancing day and night in the summer heat.

Doctors blamed "hot blood," and their cure was more dancing. The city cleared its guildhalls, built a wooden stage and hired musicians. It only made things worse; some may have danced until they collapsed. Finally, the dancers were taken to the shrine of Saint Vitus, given red shoes and crosses, and prayed over — and slowly, the dancing stopped. Similar outbreaks had struck Europe for centuries, including Aachen in 1374.

Historians such as John Waller think it was mass hysteria — a mass psychogenic illness in a city crushed by famine and disease, where fear spread from person to person until bodies obeyed.
